The spine runs top to bottom: GOES electrical steel and copper become a core & windings, which — together with tap changers and bushings — flow into OEM assembly, then ship by heavy-lift logistics to become an energised transformer. The red links are where the schedule is really set.

The GOES → core link is the single hardest constraint in the industry: roughly ten mills worldwide can make grain-oriented steel at grade, it is processing- and know-how-bound rather than ore-bound, and prices have run up 60–77% since 2020. The components → assembly link is the other red one — on-load tap changers (Reinhausen) and HV bushings (Trench) are certified per-application and served by a handful of suppliers, so one late part holds the whole build.

The dashed demand-pull arrow is not a material at all — it is the AI / data-center, grid- replacement and renewable build-out pushing transformer demand from ~1,500 toward ~9,000 units a year by 2030. It doesn't break a link; it stretches every queue behind it.

Deliberately not shown: aluminum, container freight, and iron ore. The supply-chain analysis ranked them low-impact for large power transformers — aluminum is a distribution-class winding metal, container freight doesn't move 300-tonne units, and GOES scarcity is about processing capacity, not ore. Leaving them off the map is the point.
